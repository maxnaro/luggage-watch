\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Dataset Procurement}

\subsubsection{Object Detection Dataset}

The training dataset for the object detection model was sourced from the
Microsoft COCO dataset \citep{lin2014microsoft}. As mentioned prior, the COCO
dataset is a large-scale source of over $200,000$ labelled images across 80
object categories. The entirety of the dataset would lead to redundancy with a
majority of classes being out of scope for the target application. Therefore, a
subset of the COCO dataset was created, containing only images including the
\texttt{person}, \texttt{backpack}, \texttt{handbag} and \texttt{suitcase}
classes, which I then aggregated into a single \texttt{luggage} class. This
resulted in a training dataset of $8,887$ images split into training and
validation splits of $8,397$ and $490$ images, respectively. This split ensured
that the validation set contained a representative sample of the training data,
allowing for an accurate assessment of the model's performance during training
and optimisations whilst the training set provided a sufficient amount of data
for the model to learn from.

Originally, I had intended to use datasets specific to the abandoned luggage
detection domain, such as ABODA, however these datasets failed to include the
amount of variation needed for training a robust model. Many annotated
datasets, created from the source footage, led to overfitting and poor
generalisation on test examples due to their smaller size, limited diversity in
viewpoints, and repeated scene context. The COCO dataset, while lacking the
domain specificity, offers a much larger variety of examples, covering a wide
range of environments, lighting conditions, and object appearances.

Another benefit of using the COCO dataset is the ease of access and
availability of annotations that are compatible with the latest YOLO models. A
python script was used to filter the COCO dataset, specify the YOLO annotation
format, balance class instances, remap classes to a single \texttt{luggage}
class, and oversample specific classes (\ref{sec:model_training_optimisation})
to create a more varied and proportionate training dataset.

Constructing the dataset programmatically allowed for a specific and
reproducible balance between the detected classes, which is crucial in the
context of abandoned luggage detection, where smaller objects such as bags may
be underrepresented in typical dataset. The target \texttt{person} budget was
constrained by $N_{\text{person},\text{allowed}}=r\cdot N_{\text{luggage}}$,
where $r$ is the target class instance ratio. Utilising balancing and
oversampling techniques resulted in an overall class instance ratio of
$\frac{13092}{6543} \approx 2.0:1$ between the \texttt{person} and
\texttt{luggage} classes shown in
figure~\ref{fig:dataset_class_distribution_after}; the reasoning behind this
method is covered in more detail in the model training and optimisation section
(\ref{sec:model_training_optimisation}). An example of the class distribution
of the dataset before and after the balancing and oversampling techniques were
applied is shown in figures~\ref{fig:dataset_class_distribution_before} and
\ref{fig:dataset_class_distribution_after}, respectively.

Oversampling was applied to the constituent classes of the \texttt{luggage}
class, which inferred upon review of the model's performance. This was
implemented by duplicating images containing the underrepresented classes,
defined by a parameter assigning weights to each class e.g.,
``\texttt{backpack=2,handbag=3,suitcase=1}'' for the given COCO classes. This
method allowed the model to learn the features of more difficult classes, such
as \texttt{handbag}s, which it struggled to detect in the early stages of
training.

A limitation of using the COCO dataset and the sampling techniques used is the
inherent lack of domain specificity, whilst the dataset contains a wide variety
of examples, its lack of CCTV-native footage can lead to models struggling to
generalise to the target application. Aggregating multiple classes into a
single \texttt{luggage} class can also lead to a loss of granularity in the
model's understanding of different types of luggage, however, this is a
necessary trade-off to ensure a sufficient amount of training data was
available and for the model to learn a more generalised representation of
luggage which can help with the issue of generalisation.

\subsubsection{Suspicious Luggage Dataset}
\label{sec:suspicious_luggage_dataset}

The purpose of the suspicious luggage dataset is to provide a source of data
for testing the end-to-end DeepStream pipeline, not for training the primary
detector. This dataset is important for validating the temporal logic, tracking
consistency, and alert behaviour of the system in realistic scenarios.

The coverage of the dataset was focused on scenarios relevant to the target
application, including people and luggage items that are abandoned, moving, or
in close proximity to each other. An ideal dataset would include a variety of
environments, conditions and factors such as occlusion, lighting, and crowd
density to ensure the robustness of the system. The dataset should also include
a sufficient number of examples of each scenario to allow for a comprehensive
evaluation of the system's performance as outlined in the requirements analysis
section (\ref{sec:requirements_dataset_procurement_preparation}).

A mixture of positive and negative cases were sourced to evaluate the system's
ability to correctly identify suspicious luggage while minimising false
positives; positive cases including true abandonment and negative cases
including stationary luggage with the owner nearby, brief separations, and
occlusions.

Example datasets for suspicious luggage scenarios were sourced from the ABODA
dataset (\url{https://github.com/kevinlin311tw/ABODA}), containing $11$ video
sequences of various environments and scenarios. These videos were used for the
testing and evaluation of the suspicious luggage detection pipeline, with
examples featuring marked changes in lighting condition, nighttime scenarios,
indoor and outdoor environments.

In addition, authors \citet{amin2021large} provide a dataset of scenarios split
into abandoned and non-abandoned scenarios, with graded levels of difficulty.
This dataset has been used in literature by authors
\citet{abandoned2025vrsalovic}.

Unfortunately, the PETS2006 \citep{thirde2006pets} and AVSS-2007
\citep{qmul2007avss} datasets were unable to be sourced despite their
prevalence in the literature and my efforts to contact the authors. The
utilisation of AVSS-2007 was restricted to a single example video sequence,
which was used in conjunction with the aforementioned procured datasets.

\subsection{Model Training and Optimisation}
\label{sec:model_training_optimisation}

Model training (and export, see section~\ref{sec:model_export}) was separated
into its own submodule for version control which can be found at
\url{https://github.com/maxnaro/luggage-watch-training/}.

\subsubsection{Training Environment}

Once the training dataset was procured and prepared, the next step was to train
the model and optimise its performance. The training environment involved a
containerised setup using Docker, with leveraging NVIDIA's GPU support for
accelerated training. Training, being separate to deployment, allowed for a
more flexible and powerful environment, enabling the use of the greater
computational resources of a desktop GPU and system as a whole. The hardware of
the training system can be found in the appendix (\ref{sec:training_hardware}).

The containerised approach allowed for a consistent and reproducible training
environment, with dependency management and isolation from the host system.
Containers require some overhead, but less than virtual machines, and the
benefits of consistency and ease of setup outweighed the performance cost in
this case.

The training script was implemented using Ultralytics' YOLO training framework,
which offered a high-level API for training YOLO models, built-in support for
various optimisers such as AdamW \citep{loschilov2017fixing}, and an automatic
optimiser which chooses based on other configuration parameters; data
augmentation techniques, and easy integration with the procured dataset. The
training script was used in conjunction with a custom configuration file
specifying the parameters of the training process as seen in
listing~\ref{lst:config}.

\subsubsection{Training Process and Iterative Optimisation}

A transfer learning approach was applied where a pre-trained YOLO model was
fine-tuned on the custom dataset. This approach allows the model to leverage
the general features learned from the large-scale COCO dataset, which can be
beneficial for improving performance and reducing training time, especially
when the custom dataset is relatively small. The pre-trained model used in the
final iteration was \texttt{yolo26s.pt}, a compact and efficient model
architecture, well suited for deployment on edge devices like the NVIDIA Jetson
Orin Nano.

Model training was an iterative process, with multiple rounds of training and
evaluation to optimise the model's performance. Training a YOLO model involves
adjusting various hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, number of
epochs and augmentation strategy. Feedback from validation performance was used
to guide each optimisation step, with the goal of improving mAP@0.5,
mAP@0.5:0.95, precision and recall
(section~\ref{sec:evaluation_metrics_for_object_detection}). Although all
metrics were considered, recall was prioritised because false negatives (missed
detections) are operationally more severe than false positives in suspicious
luggage detection.

An early baseline run used \texttt{yolo11l.pt} at \texttt{imgsz=640}, with
\texttt{optimizer=auto}, \texttt{lr0=0.01}, \texttt{batch=16}, no backbone
freezing, and uncached loading. A later optimised run used \texttt{yolo26s.pt}
at \texttt{imgsz=1280}, \texttt{optimizer=AdamW}, \texttt{lr0=0.001},
\texttt{batch=0.8} (auto-scaled by available VRAM), \texttt{freeze=10}, and
\texttt{cache=disk}. The rationale behind these changes was: (i) increase input
resolution to improve small-object localisation for luggage items, (ii) improve
optimisation stability with AdamW and a lower initial learning rate, (iii)
preserve transferable low-level visual features by freezing early backbone
layers, and (iv) improve training throughput and memory efficiency using disk
caching and adaptive batch sizing at higher resolution.

Another additional optimisation step that was applied was the use of early
stopping with a patience of $30$ epochs, which allows the training process to
stop early if the validation metrics do not improve for the specified number of
epochs. This can help to prevent overfitting and reduce training time. For
example, in another training run, the model was trained for $100$ epochs with
early stopping enabled, and the training process stopped at epoch $87$ when the
validation metrics plateaued (see figure ~\ref{fig:plateau_map5095}),
indicating that the model had likely converged and further training would not
yield significant improvements. This optimisation step can be particularly
beneficial when training on a limited dataset, as it helps to avoid overfitting
and ensures that the model generalises well to unseen data.

Furthermore, data augmentation techniques were applied during training to
improve the model's generalisation capabilities. These techniques included
random horizontal flipping, scaling, and colour jittering (all implemented
natively by the \texttt{auto\_augment} feature of the framework), which help
the model to learn invariant features and improve its robustness to variations
in the input data. Data mosaicing, a technique where multiple images are
combined into a single training example, was also applied to further enhance
the diversity; mosaicing was disabled in the last 10 epochs to allow the model
to fine-tune on simpler examples as it converged.

\subsection{Model Export}
\label{sec:model_export}

The result of training is a PyTorch model checkpoint file, which contains the
learned weights and architecture information. However, for deployment on the
NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, the model needed to be exported to a format compatible
with NVIDIA's TensorRT inference engine, which is optimised for performance on
proprietary hardware. The export process involved converting the PyTorch model
to ONNX format, which is a widely supported intermediate representation for
machine learning models.

Exporting the model to ONNX format was achieved using the same containerised
environment as training, utilising a custom export script sourced from
``DeepStream-Yolo'' GitHub repository \citep{luciano2026deepstreamyolo}, which
provides DeepStream SDK implementations for YOLO models. Since I had planned to
use DeepStream, and by extension \citeauthor{luciano2026deepstreamyolo}'s
\texttt{nvdsinfer} custom implementation for YOLO models, using
\citeauthor{luciano2026deepstreamyolo}'s export script ensured compatibility
and optimised performance when deploying the model.

The containerised export implementation forwards parameters into the
DeepStream-compatible YOLO26 exporter. The exporter applies model-level changes
required for compatibility with downstream DeepStream parsing: the detection
head is switched to export mode, output formatting is adapted for ONNX export,
and the final tensor is transformed into a compact per-detection layout of
\texttt{[x1, y1, x2, y2, score, class\_id]}. A \texttt{labels.txt} file is also
emitted from the trained class names to preserve class-index alignment at
inference time.

The main export configuration used in this project is summarised in the
appendix~\ref{sec:config}, with the key parameters being: \texttt{format=onnx}
to specify the export format, and \texttt{imgsz=1280} to maintain the input
resolution used during training.

In practice, this produced an ONNX model suitable for subsequent TensorRT
engine generation on the Jetson platform while retaining compatibility with the
DeepStream YOLO parser.

\subsection{Deployment Setup and Configuration}
\label{sec:deployment_setup_and_configuration}

The deployment system used an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano running JetPack 6.2 and
DeepStream 7.1 to perform real-time inference using the trained model, and
integrating the object tracking and abandonment logic into an end-to-end
pipeline.

Steps to set up the deployment environment included: (i) installing the
necessary software dependencies in order to run a Python-based DeepStream
application, (ii) compiling the custom YOLO parser library from
\citet{luciano2026deepstreamyolo} to enable support for the exported model, and
(iii) downloading the Re-ID model from NVIDIA's TAO Toolkit Model Zoo
\citep{nvidia2026reidnet}, which is used for object tracking and
re-identification in the pipeline.

Development of the inference application was done using Python, and was
contained as submodule in the repository at
\url{https://github.com/maxnaro/luggage-watch-inference} which was able to be
cloned and worked on directly on the Jetson device via SSH.

\subsection{DeepStream Pipeline Configuration}

The implementation of the DeepStream pipeline involved the instantiation of a
series of GStreamer elements, responsible for the different stages of video
processing, object detection, tracking and abandonment logic. These elements
were configured and linked together to form a cohesive pipeline that processes
video input, performs inference using the trained model, and outputs the
results in realtime. The configuration was defined programmatically in the
Python application in listing~\ref{lst:pipeline} and is represented in
figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

Iterative development and testing of the pipeline was performed using
pre-recorded video sequences from the procured suspicious luggage dataset,
which allowed for debugging of the pipeline's functionality, the generation of
TensorRT engine files, and validation of inference performance on the target
hardware before implementing the rest of the system's logic and deployment.

Configuration for the primary object detection stage of the pipeline is
determined by a custom configuration file, which specifies parameters such as
the model path, input resolution, confidence threshold, and class count. These
parameters can have a significant impact on the performance of the model and
the overall system, and were tuned based on the results of the developing and
testing process. The configuration file can be found in the appendix
~\ref{lst:primary_config}, with the reasonings for the key parameters being:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Inference Interval and Real-time Tradeoff:} The
	      \texttt{interval} parameter, which specifies the number
	      of frames to skip between inferences, was set to $3$
	      to balance the tradeoff between inference performance and real-time
	      processing. Using a lower interval (e.g., $1$) would allow for more
	      frequent detections but in practice led to increased latency and
	      reduced frame rates, while a higher interval reduced the computational
	      load at the expense of missing brief events. Ultimately, an interval
	      of $3$ was found to provide a good balance for the target application,
	      as the temporal logic of the system is designed to detect abandoned luggage over a
	      span of several seconds, so missing detections in a few frames does
	      not significantly impact the overall performance.
	\item \textbf{Aspect Ratio and Padding Behaviour:} The \texttt{maintain-aspect-ratio}
	      parameter was set to \texttt{1} (true) to ensure that the aspect ratio of the input
	      is maintained during resizing, along with the \texttt{symmetric-padding} being set
	      to \texttt{1} (true) to apply symmetric padding when resizing. This configuration was chosen to
	      preserve the spatial relationships between objects in the input frames, which is important for
	      accurate detection and tracking. Furthermore, the reduced distortion allowed the
	      model to detect smaller luggage items more effectively.
	\item \textbf{TensorRT Engine Generation and Compatibility:} The \texttt{model-engine-file}
	      parameter specifies the path to the TensorRT engine file generated from the exported ONNX model
	      specified at \texttt{onnx-file}. If no engine file can be found, the system will attempt
	      to generate one from the ONNX file which, while time-consuming, only has to be done once.
	      This engine file is optimised for inference on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, having
	      been generated on the device itself, and ensures compatibility with the DeepStream pipeline.
	      Additionally, the \texttt{network-mode} parameter is set to \texttt{2} to specify FP16
	      precision for the engine, providing the benefits outlined in
	      sections~\ref{sec:real_time_processing_on_edge_devices} and \ref{sec:tensorrt_optimisations}.
	\item \textbf{Custom Parsing Implementation for YOLO Models:} The parameters,
	      \texttt{parse-bbox-func-name}, \texttt{custom-lib-path}, and \texttt{engine-create-func-name},
	      specify the custom C++ parsing implementation for YOLO models provided by \citet{luciano2026deepstreamyolo}.
	      This enables the exported YOLO model to be correctly parsed and integrated into the DeepStream pipeline,
	      ensuring that the detections are correctly interpreted and processed by the subsequent elements in the pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Object Tracking Integration}

Object tracking is a crucial component of the system, allowing for the
consistent identification of objects across frames, which is essential for the
temporal logic of abandoned luggage detection. The tracking component was
implemented as a \texttt{nvtracker} element of the pipeline using the
\texttt{NvDeepSORT} algorithm.

\subsubsection{Determining the Optimal Tracking Algorithm}

The choice of tracking algorithm was informed by a combination of researching
current implementations
(\ref{sec:video_analysis_spatio_temporal_modeling_and_object_tracking}),
determining the solutions used by similar projects in the literature (e.g.,
ByteTrack \citep{zhang2022bytetrack} used by \citet{abandoned2025vrsalovic}),
and practical testing of different algorithms on the target hardware. Luckily,
the modular design of the DeepStream pipeline allowed for relatively easy
integration and testing of different tracking algorithms, as they can be
swapped out as different configurations passed to the \texttt{nvtracker}
element.

The available options from the DeepStream SDK included: \texttt{IoU Tracker},
using the IoU values among detections to associate them across frames;
\texttt{NvSORT}, an implementation of the SORT algorithm
\citep{bewley2016simple} which uses a combination of bounding box proximities
for association and a Kalman filter to update the state of tracked objects;
\texttt{NvDeepSORT}, an extension of SORT that incorporates a deep association
metric \citep{wojke2017simple} and a Re-ID model for association;
\texttt{NvDCF}, an implementation that uses a discriminative correlation filter
for tracking; and \texttt{MaskTracker}, mainly used for segmentation tasks.

After testing the different algorithms, it became clear that the requirements
of the system required a robust tracking solution. Environmental factors such
as occlusion and crowd density whilst using a less rigorous tracking algorithm
led to frequent ID flips and ownership misattribution, which in turn led to
incorrect abandonment detections. The requirements for ownership and ID
consistency across frames, made \texttt{NvDeepSORT} the most suitable choice,
as its use of a deep association metric and Re-ID model provided improved
robustness to occlusion and appearance changes, which are common in
surveillance scenarios.

Utilising the pretrained Re-ID model \citep{nvidia2026reidnet}, based on the
ResNet 50 architecture, the tracking component was able to maintain consistent
IDs for objects across frames - even in the face of occlusion or temporary
disappearance. This enabled the temporal logic of the system to accurately
determine when a piece of luggage had been abandoned, and reliably track the
owner of the luggage given they were still in the scene.

\subsubsection{Intercepting Tracking Outputs}

The implementation of the tracking component also involved intercepting the
outputs of the DeepSORT algorithm. This used \texttt{GStreamer} pad probes as
the mechanism, attaching a \texttt{Gst.PadProbeType.BUFFER} probe to the output
pad of the \texttt{nvtracker} element. This meant that the tracking outputs
could be accessed and processed in real-time as they were generated by the
tracker, thus, giving access to post-tracking metadata before downstream
elements in the pipeline.

The tracker probe acts as an entrypoint for the abandonment detection logic,
splitting the tracking outputs (objects and IDs) into separate lists for person
and luggage classes, piping that information into the processing function,
where the OSD metadata and final outputs are produced.

\subsection{Abandonment Detection Logic}

The logic for abandonment can be split into two main components: (i) processing
each frame's tracking outputs, iterating through the detected luggage items and
determining their environmental contexts; (ii) maintaining the state of each
luggage item across frame, by retaining a context object for each item -
tracking its ID, the ID of its owner (if any), its state (e.g., attended,
unattended, abandoned) , its last bounding box, and the (monotonically
increasing) timestamp of its last update.

\subsubsection{Processing Tracking Outputs}

The parameters passed to the processing function by the tracker probe include a
detected persons and luggage items, in the form of two lists of
\texttt{NvDsObjectMeta} objects. Each object contains information such as
bounding box parameters, confidence score, class ID, object (tracking) ID, and
OSD metadata. A global dictionary is used to maintain the context of each
detected luggage item across frames, the tracking ID of the luggage item is
used as the key, and a \texttt{LuggageContext} object is used as the value.

Two classes are used to abstract from the raw metadata and represent the
detected objects' contexts and bounding boxes in a more structured way:
\texttt{LuggageContext} and \texttt{BBox}. A third set of derived classes,
\texttt{State}, is used to represent the state of a luggage item.

The three states, \texttt{Attended}, \texttt{Unattended} and
\texttt{Abandoned}, are derived from a single base class, and represent the
different states of a luggage item. Each implements an \texttt{evaluate} method
which, given a \texttt{LuggageContext} object, determines whether the luggage
item meets the criteria for transitioning states. This object-oriented approach
can be seen in the class diagram in figure~\ref{fig:states_class_diagram}.

\begin{figure*}[!p]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/plantuml/states_class_diagram.png}
	\caption{Class diagram representing the object-oriented implementation of the luggage state machine.}
	\label{fig:states_class_diagram}
\end{figure*}

The \texttt{LuggageContext} implementation functions as a per-object memory
that bridges frame-wise tracker outputs and state-based abandonment logic. For
each tracked luggage ID, the context stores persistent attributes (owner
hypothesis, last observed bounding box, and current state), then updates them
once per frame using a fixed evaluation order: movement estimation from
inter-frame box displacement, attendance estimation from owner proximity (or
nearest-owner assignment when no owner is set), and delegation of transition
decisions to the active state object. This ordering ensures deterministic
behaviour and clear responsibility boundaries between context management and
transition policy. The nearest-owner assignment step is radius-constrained to
reduce spurious associations in crowded scenes, at the cost of occasionally
losing ownership under heavy occlusion. Overall, this context-centric design
provides a practical implementation of temporal reasoning while keeping
state-transition logic modular and extensible.

This encompasses the first component of the abandonment logic, processing of
each frame's metadata into a structured context and evaluating the state of
each luggage item based on its current context and the defined criteria for
each state's transitions.

\subsubsection{Context Lifecycle Management}
\label{sec:context_lifecycle}

\paragraph{Original lifecycle.}

In the initial design, each context object was created when a new luggage
tracking ID appeared and deleted when that ID had not been seen for a fixed
time-to-live (TTL). This was simple and sufficient when tracker IDs were
stable, but brittle when the tracker reassigned IDs, a common occurrence in
crowded scenes where occlusions cause a luggage item to lose and regain its
track. Under the original scheme, an ID reassignment would discard all
accumulated state (owner, timers, state machine position) and start a fresh
context, resetting any progress towards abandonment detection.

\paragraph{State-dependent TTL.}

The fixed TTL has been replaced with a state-dependent scheme. Normal contexts
expire after \texttt{CONTEXT\_TTL\_SECONDS} (default~12\,s) of undetection.
Abandoned contexts use a longer TTL of
\texttt{ABANDONED\_CONTEXT\_TTL\_SECONDS} (default~45\,s). This asymmetry is
deliberate: an abandoned luggage item that temporarily leaves the tracker
output (due to occlusion or a dropped frame) should not lose its accumulated
state --- the operational value of maintaining an abandonment alert outweighs
the memory cost of a longer-lived context.

\paragraph{Context relinking.}

When a new luggage ID appears with no existing context, the system does not
immediately create a fresh context. Instead, it searches existing contexts
whose IDs are no longer present in the current frame for a possible match. A
candidate context must satisfy all of the following eligibility criteria:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Missing duration:} the context's ID must have been absent for
	      no longer than a state-dependent window (default~4\,s for normal
	      contexts, 8\,s for abandoned).
	\item \textbf{Spatial distance:} the new bounding box must be within a
	      state-dependent tolerance of the context's last known position
	      (default~100\,px for normal contexts, 180\,px for abandoned).
	\item \textbf{Area ratio:} the ratio of the larger bounding box area to the
	      smaller must not exceed a configurable threshold (default~2.5),
	      preventing relinking when the new detection differs substantially in
	      size from the original.
\end{itemize}

When multiple candidates qualify, the system selects the best match using a
$(\textit{distance},\; \textit{missing\_age},\; \textit{area\_ratio})$ scoring
tuple, preferring the spatially closest, most recently seen, and most similarly
sized context. The matched context is then transferred from the old ID to the
new one, preserving all accumulated state: owner hypothesis, state machine
position, timers, and confirmation counters. If no match is found, a fresh
context is created.

Abandoned contexts receive wider spatial and temporal gates for relinking,
reflecting the higher cost of losing an abandonment detection compared to
creating a spurious new context. Lifecycle events (creation, relinking, expiry)
are exposed through an optional debug callback, used during development and
evaluation to trace context behaviour without impacting pipeline performance.

\subsubsection{Ownership Assignment and Re-identification}
\label{sec:ownership_assignment}

The initial implementation of ownership assignment used a single-frame,
centre-to-centre nearest-person heuristic. For each unowned luggage item, the
nearest person was selected if and only if the Euclidean distance between
bounding box centres fell within a fixed radius threshold. Formally, for
luggage box $b_l$, person boxes $\{b_p\}$, $d(x, y)$ denoting the Euclidean
distance between centre points of $x$ and $y$, and attendance radius $R_a$:
\begin{align}
	\text{owner}(l) = \argmin_p{d(b_l, b_p) \text{ s.t. } d(b_l, b_p) \leq R_a}.
\end{align}
If no candidate satisfied the radius constraint, no owner was assigned. This
baseline was computationally lightweight and sufficient for early pipeline
testing. However, single-frame assignment proved unstable under tracker noise:
a one-frame detection glitch could instantly assign the wrong owner, and the
system had no mechanism to recover from misattribution or tolerate brief
tracking dropouts. These limitations motivated a series of refinements to the
ownership logic, described below.

\paragraph{Edge-aware proximity.}

The original proximity check used only the Euclidean distance between bounding
box centres. This can misrepresent the physical relationship between a person
and their luggage: when a person stands directly beside or over a bag, the
centre-to-centre distance may be large even though the luggage is clearly
within the person's spatial footprint. To address this, the proximity check was
replaced with a two-tier test. The first tier computes the shortest distance
from the luggage centre to the nearest point on the person bounding box area (a
clamped projection), denoted $d_{\text{edge}}$:
\begin{align}
	d_{\text{edge}}(b_l, b_p) = \left\lVert \mathbf{c}_l - \text{clamp}(\mathbf{c}_l, b_p) \right\rVert_2
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{c}_l$ is the luggage centre and
$\text{clamp}(\mathbf{c}_l, b_p)$ projects $\mathbf{c}_l$ onto the nearest
point within the axis-aligned extent of $b_p$. If
$d_{\text{edge}} \leq R_{\text{edge}}$ (default $80$~px), the person is
considered within range. Otherwise, the system falls back to the centre-to-centre
distance $d(b_l, b_p) \leq R_a$ (default $200$~px). This two-tier check
improves sensitivity to close-proximity ownership while retaining the broader
radius for more distant associations.

\paragraph{Multi-frame confirmation.}

Rather than committing an owner on a single frame, the system now requires the
same candidate to be the best match for $N_{\text{assign}}$ consecutive frames
(default $3$) before the assignment is finalised. Each frame, the best
candidate is selected using a composite key of $(d_{\text{edge}},\,
	d_{\text{centre}})$, preferring candidates whose bounding box is physically
closest to the luggage. If the best candidate changes before confirmation is
reached, the confirmation counter resets. This filters out transient
associations caused by tracker jitter or brief person crossings near the
luggage.

\paragraph{Owner persistence and consistency checking.}

Once assigned, the owner ID remains persistent across frames, carried forward
from the original design. However, the system now validates the owner each
frame by checking spatial consistency: if the owner's bounding box shifts by
more than a configured threshold (default $120$~px) between consecutive frames,
the system treats the ID as unreliable. This guards against person-tracker ID
flips, where the tracker reassigns the same numeric ID to a different physical
person. When an inconsistency is detected, the system enters the missing-owner
path rather than accepting the shifted trajectory.

When the owner disappears from the tracking output entirely, attendance is
maintained for a configurable grace period (default $2$~s). This prevents a
single missed detection frame from immediately triggering the unattended
pathway, tolerating brief detector dropouts and partial occlusions without
fragmenting the temporal history.

\paragraph{Owner reassignment for person-tracker ID flips.}

When the owner goes missing or fails the consistency check, the system attempts
controlled reassignment within a time window (default $15$~s). A reassignment
candidate must satisfy three conditions: (i) it must be within the attendance
range of the luggage (using the same two-tier proximity check), (ii) its
position must be spatially close to the owner's last known bounding box
(default $\leq 140$~px shift), and (iii) it must persist as the best candidate
for $N_{\text{reassign}}$ consecutive frames (default $3$). Candidate scoring
uses a $(d_{\text{shift}},\, d_{\text{edge}},\, d_{\text{centre}})$ tuple,
prioritising candidates that most closely resemble the original owner's
position. If no candidate qualifies within the reassignment window, the owner
is considered lost and the luggage falls through to the unattended pathway.

\paragraph{Owner history as an abandonment guard.}

The context tracks whether an owner has ever been confirmed for a given luggage
item. An owner is marked as confirmed once it has been observed within
attendance range for a minimum duration (default $0.5$~s, accumulated across
frames). This flag is consumed by the state machine: when enabled, luggage
cannot transition to the Abandoned state unless an owner was confirmed at some
point. This prevents newly appeared objects that were never associated with an
owner (e.g., a bag placed by someone outside the camera's field of view) from
immediately triggering false abandonment alerts. When no owner history exists,
the abandonment timeout is extended by a configurable multiplier (default
$3\times$), providing additional time for an owner to be detected before
escalation.

The full per-frame ownership evaluation, incorporating the refinements
described above, is summarised in
algorithm~\ref{alg:ownership_evaluation_impl}.

\begin{algorithm*}[!htb]
	\caption{Per-frame ownership evaluation (implementation, replacing~\ref{alg:association}).}
	\label{alg:ownership_evaluation_impl}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require Luggage context $l$ with fields $l.bbox$, $l.owner$, $l.owner\_bbox$, $l.missing\_since$, $l.candidate$, $l.candidate\_frames$; tracked persons set $P$; current time $t$
		\Ensure $attended$ as attendance result for this frame
		\If{$l.owner = null$} \Comment{No owner yet $\implies$ attempt initial assignment}
		\State $best\_key \gets (\infty, \infty)$, $best\_person \gets null$
		\ForAll{$p \in P$}
		\State $d_e \gets \Call{EdgeDist}{l.bbox, p.bbox}$ \Comment{Luggage centre to person box area}
		\State $d_c \gets \Call{CentreDist}{l.bbox, p.bbox}$ \Comment{Centre-to-centre distance}
		\If{$(d_e \leq R_{\text{edge}} \vee d_c \leq R_a) \wedge (d_e, d_c) < best\_key$}
		\State $best\_key \gets (d_e, d_c)$, $best\_person \gets p$
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\If{$\neg\Call{ConfirmCandidate}{l, best\_person, N_{\text{assign}}}$} \Return \textsc{False} \EndIf
		\State $l.owner \gets best\_person$, $l.owner\_bbox \gets best\_person.bbox$
		\State $l.missing\_since \gets null$\\
		\Return \textsc{True}
		\EndIf
		\Statex
		\If{$l.owner \in P$} \Comment{Owner visible in tracking output}
		\State $d_{\text{shift}} \gets \Call{CentreDist}{l.owner.bbox, l.owner\_bbox}$
		\If{$d_{\text{shift}} \leq S_{\max}$} \Comment{Trajectory is spatially consistent}
		\State $l.owner\_bbox \gets l.owner.bbox$, $l.missing\_since \gets null$\\
		\Return $\Call{InRange}{l.bbox, l.owner.bbox}$ \Comment{Two-tier proximity}
		\Else \Comment{Trajectory jump $\implies$ treat owner as missing}
		\If{$l.missing\_since = null$} \State $l.missing\_since \gets t$ \EndIf
		\EndIf
		\Else \Comment{Owner not in tracking output}
		\If{$l.missing\_since = null$} \State $l.missing\_since \gets t$ \EndIf
		\EndIf
		\Statex
		\If{$t - l.missing\_since \leq W_{\text{reassign}}$} \Comment{Within reassignment window}
		\State $c \gets \Call{ReassignCandidate}{l.bbox, l.owner\_bbox, P}$
		\If{$\Call{ConfirmCandidate}{l, c, N_{\text{reassign}}}$}
		\State $l.owner \gets c$, $l.owner\_bbox \gets c.bbox$, $l.missing\_since \gets null$\\
		\Return $\Call{InRange}{l.bbox, c.bbox}$
		\EndIf
		\EndIf\\
		\Return $t - l.missing\_since \leq G_{\text{grace}}$ \Comment{Grace period}
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

\paragraph{Limitations.}

The ownership logic remains fundamentally a proximity-based heuristic rather
than an interaction-based one. In dense scenes the nearest person may not be
the true owner, and for scenes of varying depth, fixed pixel thresholds may not
be optimal as the apparent size of objects and their spatial relationships
change with distance from the camera. The mitigations described above
(multi-frame confirmation, consistency checking, and controlled reassignment)
reduce the frequency and impact of misattribution, but cannot eliminate it
entirely. More advanced approaches such as hand-object contact modelling or
depth-aware distance normalisation remain directions for future work.

\paragraph{Complexity.}

Per frame, initial ownership assignment retains linear complexity in the number
of detected people for each luggage item, giving $O(n \cdot m)$ for $n$ luggage
items and $m$ people. Consistency checking and confirmation counters add $O(1)$
per luggage item. Reassignment, when triggered, performs a constant-bounded
scan over people (gated by the reassignment time window), so the overall
worst-case complexity remains $O(n \cdot m)$.

\subsubsection{Movement and Attendance Heuristics}

\paragraph{Movement.}

Movement detection is performed by computing the inter-frame displacement of
each luggage bounding box's centre point. If the displacement exceeds a
configurable threshold, the luggage item is classified as moving; otherwise it
is classified as stationary. Formally, for luggage box $b_l^{t}$ at frame $t$
and $b_l^{t-1}$ at frame $t-1$:
\begin{equation}
	m_t = \begin{cases}
		\text{True}  & \text{if } d(b_l^{t}, b_l^{t-1}) \ge \tau_m \\
		\text{False} & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_m$ is the movement threshold (a fixed pixel value, default~25~px,
configurable at runtime) and $d(x, y)$ denotes the Euclidean distance between
centre points of $x$ and $y$. This simple heuristic avoids overfitting to
accumulated tracking noise and provides a clear empirical signal for
distinguishing intentional motion from detection jitter. On the first frame of
a newly detected luggage item, no prior bounding box is available, so
\texttt{is\_moving} defaults to True --- this prevents newly appeared objects
from immediately satisfying the unattended entry condition.

\paragraph{Original attendance heuristic.}

The initial attendance check was a single centre-to-centre proximity test. If
an owner had been assigned and was currently tracked, attendance was True when
the distance remained within a configurable radius:
\begin{equation}
	a_t = \begin{cases}
		d(b_l, b_p^{\text{owner}}) \le R_a
		 & \text{if owner visible} \\[1ex]
		\text{False}
		 & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $R_a$ is the attendance radius and $b_p^{\text{owner}}$ the owner's
bounding box. When no owner had been assigned, the assignment step implicitly
performed an attendance check: if no person was within the radius, the luggage
was unattended. This formulation was sufficient for early testing but did not
account for edge-aware proximity, grace periods during tracker dropouts, or
owner consistency checking --- all of which now feed into the attendance signal.

\paragraph{Updated attendance signal.}

In the current implementation, \texttt{is\_attended} is the output of
\texttt{\_evaluate\_owner\_attendance}, which evaluates one of several paths
depending on the ownership state of the luggage item:
\begin{equation}
	a_t = \begin{cases}
		\textsc{InRange}(b_l, b_o)
		 & \text{owner visible} \\[1ex]
		\textsc{InRange}(b_l, b_{o'})
		 & \text{reassigned}    \\[1ex]
		t - t_{\text{miss}} \le T_g
		 & \text{owner missing} \\[1ex]
		\textsc{Assign}(b_l, P)
		 & \text{no owner}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\textsc{InRange}$ is the two-tier proximity
check from section~\ref{sec:ownership_assignment}
($d_{\text{edge}} \le R_{\text{edge}}$ or $d \le R_a$), $T_g$ is the
owner-lost grace period (default~2\,s), $t_{\text{miss}}$ the timestamp at
which the owner was last seen, and \textsc{Assign} returns True if initial
owner assignment succeeds.

The attendance signal is therefore no longer a pure spatial predicate: it
incorporates temporal state via the grace period and ownership lifecycle logic
via the assignment and reassignment paths. This means that
\texttt{is\_attended} can be True even when the assigned owner is not currently
visible in the tracking output, provided the grace period has not yet expired.
In practice, this prevents brief detector dropouts from immediately triggering
the unattended pathway.

\paragraph{Signal decoupling.}

By computing movement and attendance as independent signals, the state machine
can distinguish between transient motion (wind, tracking artefacts, gentle
nudging) and intentional carrying or retrieval by an owner. The richer
attendance signal --- with its grace period and consistency checking ---
further stabilises this decoupling: brief tracker dropouts no longer cause
attendance to flicker between frames, reducing noise in the input to the
hysteresis mechanism described in
section~\ref{sec:state_transition_invariants}.

\paragraph{Parameter sensitivity.}

The movement threshold $\tau_m$ and the attendance radii ($R_{\text{edge}}$ and
$R_a$) are configuration constants that require tuning based on expected scene
characteristics. A strict (small) movement threshold increases sensitivity to
abandonment but risks false positives from environmental motion or gradual
settling; a lax threshold may miss subtle repositioning. For the attendance
radii, the edge radius $R_{\text{edge}}$ governs close-proximity detection (a
person standing directly beside their luggage), while the broader centre radius
$R_a$ covers more distant associations. Tightening either radius improves
precision but risks rapid owner loss under occlusion or crowding; widening them
tolerates dense scenarios at the cost of potential misattribution. The values
used in development prioritise recall over precision to minimise missed
detections in operational deployment.

\subsubsection{State Transition Invariants}
\label{sec:state_transition_invariants}

The state machine enforces a set of structural invariants to ensure
deterministic and stable behaviour.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Single Active State:} At any time, a luggage item has exactly
	      one active state from the set \{\texttt{Attended}, \texttt{Unattended},
	      \texttt{Abandoned}\}. State updates are performed with explicit
	      transitions, so simultaneous multi-state activation is not possible.
	\item \textbf{Adjacency Invariance:} Transitions can only occur between
	      adjacent states in the defined state transition graph
	      (figure~\ref{fig:state_machine}). No direct transition from
	      \texttt{Attended} to \texttt{Abandoned} is possible without first
	      passing through \texttt{Unattended}.
	\item \textbf{Guard-conditioned Transitions:} Each transition is triggered by
	      specific conditions evaluated in the context of the luggage item.
	\item \textbf{Persistence of Abandonment:} Once in the \texttt{Abandoned}
	      state, the object remains abandoned.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Original transition function.}

The initial design defined transitions as single-frame predicates: let $s_t$
denote the state at frame~$t$, $a_t$ attendance, $m_t$ movement, and $\Delta
	t_u$ the elapsed time since becoming unattended with timeout~$T_a$:

\begin{small}
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:original_transition}
		s_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
			\text{Unatt} & \text{if } s_t = \text{Att} \wedge \neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t               \\
			\text{Att}   & \text{if } s_t = \text{Unatt} \wedge (a_t \vee m_t)                       \\
			\text{Abnd}  & \begin{aligned}[t]
				               \text{if } s_t & = \text{Unatt} \wedge \neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t \\[-0.5ex]
				                              & \quad \wedge \Delta t_u \ge T_a
			               \end{aligned} \\
			\text{Abnd}  & \text{if } s_t = \text{Abnd}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
\end{small}

Under this formulation, every transition was decided on a single frame of
evidence: one frame of $\neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t$ was sufficient to begin the
unattended path, and one frame of $a_t \vee m_t$ could immediately reset the
abandonment timer. In practice, a single spurious detection could flip the
state, and a momentary attendance flicker caused by tracker noise could reset
the timer entirely. This made the state machine vulnerable to the noisy output
of the object tracker, motivating the introduction of multi-frame confirmation.

\paragraph{Hysteresis via multi-frame confirmation.}

To resist rapid oscillation between states, the implementation introduces a
frame-level hysteresis mechanism. Rather than reacting to single-frame signals,
the state machine accumulates evidence over consecutive frames and only commits
to a transition once a configurable threshold is reached. Two per-luggage
counters are maintained in \texttt{LuggageContext.update}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Entry counter}
	      (\texttt{\_unattended\_entry\_frames}): incremented each frame where
	      $\neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t$ holds while in the \texttt{Attended} state;
	      reset to zero if either condition breaks. The \texttt{Attended} state
	      only transitions to \texttt{Unattended} once this counter reaches
	      \texttt{UNATTENDED\_ENTRY\_CONFIRM\_FRAMES} (default~3).
	\item \textbf{Reset counter}
	      (\texttt{\_unattended\_reset\_frames}): incremented each frame where
	      $a_t$ holds while in the \texttt{Unattended} state; reset to zero
	      otherwise. The \texttt{Unattended} state returns to \texttt{Attended}
	      via attendance only once this counter reaches
	      \texttt{UNATTENDED\_RESET\_CONFIRM\_FRAMES} (default~3).
\end{itemize}

Movement of the luggage ($m_t$) bypasses this hysteresis entirely: a single
frame of movement in the \texttt{Unattended} state triggers an immediate return
to \texttt{Attended}. This asymmetry is intentional --- physical displacement
of the bounding box is a strong signal unlikely to be a tracker artefact,
whereas proximity of a person can fluctuate due to tracker noise or brief
occlusion.

The \texttt{State.evaluate} methods themselves only read the resulting boolean
properties (\texttt{unattended\_entry\_confirmed},
\texttt{unattended\_reset\_confirmed}); the counting logic is centralised in
\texttt{LuggageContext.update}, keeping it state-independent and consistent.

\paragraph{Abandonment guards.}

The \texttt{Unattended}~$\to$~\texttt{Abandoned} transition is additionally
gated by two guards that extend beyond the base timeout:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Owner history:} When
	      \texttt{REQUIRE\_OWNER\_HISTORY\_FOR\_ABANDONED} is enabled (default),
	      luggage cannot transition to \texttt{Abandoned} unless an owner was
	      confirmed at some point (\texttt{owner\_confirmed\_once}). This
	      prevents newly appeared, never-owned objects from triggering false
	      alarms. In addition, when no owner was ever confirmed the base
	      timeout~$T_a$ is multiplied by
	      \texttt{OWNERLESS\_ABANDONMENT\_TIMEOUT\_MULTIPLIER} (default~$3\times$),
	      extending the default 30\,s window to 90\,s and giving the system more
	      time to establish ownership before escalating. It was important to include
	      this as a configurable guard rather than a hard requirement,
	      as in some scenarios ownership may or may not be needed for an object to be
	      considered abandoned. For example, two separate test sets, ABODA and IITP20
	      (section~\ref{sec:suspicious_luggage_dataset}) have conflicting
	      definitions of abandonment with respect to ownership, so this guard allows the
	      system to be adapted to different contexts without code changes.
	\item \textbf{Minimum context age:} The luggage context must have existed for
	      at least \texttt{MIN\_CONTEXT\_AGE\_FOR\_ABANDONED\_SECONDS}
	      (default~3\,s) before abandonment is possible. This filters out
	      transient detections that appear and immediately satisfy the unattended
	      condition.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unattended timer resume.}

Complementing the frame-level hysteresis, a temporal hysteresis is applied to
the abandonment timer itself. When luggage transitions from
\texttt{Unattended}~$\to$~\texttt{Attended} and then re-enters
\texttt{Unattended} within \texttt{UNATTENDED\_TIMER\_RESUME\_WINDOW\_SECONDS}
(default~6\,s), the timer resumes from its previous elapsed value rather than
restarting from zero. On exit from \texttt{Unattended}, the elapsed time and
exit timestamp are saved; on re-entry within the window, the new state's entry
time is backdated to preserve continuity. If the gap exceeds the window, a
fresh timer starts. Together, the two layers of hysteresis, frame-level
confirmation and timer-level resume, prevent both single-frame noise from
causing spurious transitions and repeated brief interactions from indefinitely
resetting the abandonment countdown.

\paragraph{Updated transition function.}

Incorporating the mechanisms above, the transition function becomes: let $C(k,
	N)$ denote that predicate~$k$ has held for at least $N$~consecutive frames, and
let $G(l)$ be the conjunction of abandonment guards for luggage~$l$:

\begin{small}
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:updated_transition}
		s_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
			\text{Unatt} & \text{if } s_t = \text{Att} \wedge C(\neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t,\; N_e)           \\[1ex]
			\text{Att}   & \text{if } s_t = \text{Unatt} \wedge m_t                                        \\
			\text{Att}   & \text{if } s_t = \text{Unatt} \wedge C(a_t,\; N_r)                              \\[1ex]
			\text{Abnd}  & \begin{aligned}[t]
				               \text{if } s_t & = \text{Unatt} \wedge \neg a_t \wedge \neg m_t       \\[-0.5ex]
				                              & \quad \wedge\; \Delta t_u \ge T_a(l) \;\wedge\; G(l)
			               \end{aligned} \\[1ex]
			\text{Abnd}  & \text{if } s_t = \text{Abnd}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
\end{small}

\noindent where $N_e$ and $N_r$ are the entry and reset confirmation thresholds
respectively, and:
\begin{equation}
	T_a(l) = \begin{cases}
		T_a \cdot k_o & \text{if } \neg\, \textit{owner\_conf} \\
		T_a           & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		G(l) ={} & \bigl(\textit{owner\_conf}
		\;\vee\; \neg\,\textit{req\_hist}\bigr) \\
		         & \wedge\;
		\bigl(\textit{ctx\_age} \ge T_{\min}\bigr)
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $k_o$ the ownerless timeout multiplier,
\textit{owner\_conf} short for \texttt{owner\_confirmed\_once}, and
\textit{req\_hist} for \texttt{REQUIRE\_OWNER\_HISTORY\_FOR\_ABANDONED}.

\subsubsection{Alert Generation}

The alert generation stage converts the state-machine outputs into
operator-visible signals in real time. After the tracking metadata is
intercepted by the probe callback, and the state of each luggage item is
evaluated, the summaries are passed to a method to update the OSD metadata in
order to visualise the state of each luggage item on the output video stream.
This is implemented across \texttt{probes.py}, \texttt{process.py}, and
\texttt{overlay.py}.

Alerts are derived from the finite-state machine output, rather than a separate
classification head. In practice, the current luggage state acts as the alert
level: \texttt{Attended} corresponds to no alert, \texttt{Unattended}
corresponds to a warning, and \texttt{Abandoned} corresponds to an actionable
alert. The OSD encodes this mapping through colour-coding and text annotations,
with attended luggage outlined in green, unattended luggage outlined in yellow,
and abandoned luggage outlined in red. Additionally, the OSD displays the
ownership radius and a straight line ``tether'' connecting the luggage to its
assigned owner when attended, providing a visual cue of the ownership
relationship and proximity.

Because alert emission depends on the guards defined in the state machine (see
section~\ref{sec:state_transition_invariants}), brief detector noise doesn't
immediately escalate to critical alerts.

The current implementation prioritises immediate visual alerting through the
OSD. It does not yet emit persistent external alerts (e.g., via message bus
events, structured logs, or notification webhooks) beyond development console
output. An extension is to trigger one-shot events on state transitions, which
could be consumed by external monitoring systems or operator dashboards for
enhanced situational awareness and response coordination.

\subsubsection{Computational Complexity and Runtime Cost}

The abandonment logic is designed to remain computationally lightweight on edge
hardware by combining constant-time geometric checks with bounded linear scans.
Let $L$ be the number of tracked luggage items, $P$ the number of tracked
people, $T$ the number of active luggage contexts, and $O$ the total number of
tracked objects in a frame.

In the processing stage, person bounding boxes are first materialised in
$O(P)$. For each luggage item, context update combines (i) movement and
attendance checks that are constant-time when an owner hypothesis already
exists, and (ii) nearest-owner assignment that is linear in people in the worst
case, giving $O(P)$ per unassigned luggage item. Tracker-flip handling
introduces an additional scan over existing contexts, giving up to $O(T)$ per
luggage item in the worst case. Context garbage collection for lost IDs is
linear in retained contexts, $O(T)$.

Combining these terms gives a worst-case per-frame bound for the abandonment
logic of:
\begin{equation}
	O(LT + LP + P + T)
\end{equation}
which is commonly simplified to $O(L(T+P))$ for moderate-to-large
object counts. In practical steady-state operation, average cost is typically
lower because many luggage items retain a valid owner and therefore bypass the
full nearest-neighbour scan.

The overlay stage remains linear in frame object count. Constructing the person
centre map is $O(P)$, iterating tracked objects for text/box updates is $O(O)$,
and ownership line/circle drawing is $O(1)$ per attended luggage item (with
bounded metadata pool management). Memory complexity is dominated by persistent
context storage, $O(T)$, plus per-frame temporary maps $O(P+L)$.

One implementation detail affects realised runtime: frame-level processing and
overlay updates are currently called from inside the object-iteration loop in
the probe callback. This multiplies effective work within a frame and increases
constant factors compared with a single invocation per frame. Refactoring those
calls to execute once after object collection would preserve the asymptotic
bound while reducing practical runtime overhead.

\subsubsection{Failure Modes and Mitigation Strategies}
\label{sec:failure_modes_and_mitigation_strategies}

The main failure modes of the abandonment detection logic include: tracker ID
flips, ownership misattribution, and tracker ID loss.

\paragraph{Tracker ID flips.}

A common failure mode in crowded scenes is when the tracker assigns a new ID to
the same physical luggage item after occlusion or brief overlap. Without
mitigation, this would reset the luggage context and could cause premature or
missed abandonment decisions. To reduce this, the processing stage performs
spatial continuity checks when a new luggage ID is observed: if the new box is
within a tolerance radius of a recently seen context, the existing context is
transferred to the new ID. This preserves state, owner hypothesis, and temporal
history across ID changes.

\paragraph{Ownership misattribution.}

Ownership is inferred using nearest-person proximity with a fixed radius gate.
In dense scenes this can assign the wrong owner, especially when multiple
people are co-located or crossing paths near luggage. The current mitigation is
conservative gating: ownership is only assigned when candidates are
sufficiently close, and owner identity is then kept persistent rather than
re-selected every frame. This reduces rapid owner switching, but can still fail
under prolonged crowding or strong perspective effects.

\paragraph{Temporary tracker loss.}

Detector misses, partial occlusion, and motion blur can cause short gaps in
tracking output. If contexts were removed immediately, these gaps would
fragment the temporal history and increase false alerts. The implementation
therefore uses time-to-live (TTL) context retention, where missing IDs are only
removed after a grace period. This supports re-association after short dropouts
while preventing unbounded growth of stale contexts.

\paragraph{Boundary sensitivity of fixed thresholds.}

Attendance, movement, and tracker-flip association all rely on pixel
thresholds. Scenes with varying depth, camera angle, or frame resolution can
shift object scale and therefore change the effective meaning of these
thresholds. The practical mitigation is calibration by scenario (e.g.,
concourse versus corridor footage) and prioritisation of recall-oriented
settings during safety-critical testing.

\paragraph{Visual ambiguity for operators.}

Even when state estimation is correct, operators may misinterpret why a state
was assigned if overlays are sparse. To improve explainability, the OSD shows
object ID, state label, colour-coded box severity, and (for attended luggage) a
line plus ownership radius overlay. These cues make the underlying decision
basis more transparent during live monitoring.

Despite these mitigations, limitations remain. The current system does not use
interaction modelling (for example, hand-object contact), depth-aware distance
normalisation, or global multi-frame assignment optimisation. These are
promising directions for future work to further reduce false positives and
identity drift in highly crowded scenes.
